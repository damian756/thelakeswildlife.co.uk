Fieldfare
Turdus pilaris Group: Thrushes UK Conservation status: Red
How to identify
Fieldfares are large, colourful thrushes, much like a Mistle Thrush in size, shape and behaviour. They stand very upright and move forward with purposeful hops. They are very social birds, spending the winter in flocks of anything from ten or twenty to several hundred strong. These straggling, chuckling flocks which roam the UK's countryside are a delightful and attractive part of the winter scene.

Fieldfare
Niels Krabbe / xeno-canto
Key features to look out for
Large thrush bigger than a Blackbird (25cm).
A blue grey head and rump, brown above and pale and speckled below with a sunset-orange chest patch.
Listen for their chuckling ‘chack-chack' chattering.
Fieldfare
Did you know? They are rather noisy bird, the loud and excited ‘chack-chack’ calls often alert you to their presence, as they explode from a tall hawthorn or pass overhead in search of their next berry-filled bush.
Conservation status
Globally, Fieldfares are listed as ‘least concern’. However, in the UK, our small breeding population of Fieldfares is red-listed on the UK Birds of Conservation Concern following a strong decline between 1996-2019. Research is ongoing to work out the cause of their decline.
Where and when to see them
Difficulty level - Medium
Fieldfares join mixed flocks with Redwings and can be seen coming into roost together at dusk. Look out for these birds perched at the tops of trees with other thrushes in local parks, countryside and nature reserves. They can be identified by their very ‘upright’ silhouette.

Behaviour
Fieldfares are one of our largest thrush species, and just like their dainty Redwing counterparts, are winter visitors on UK shores. These colourful birds start arriving from Scandinavia, Western Russia and Eastern Europe from October. From then, they can be seen roaming our countryside and green spaces in gossiping, berry-hungry flocks until April.
Despite the size difference and shared love of berries, Fieldfares get along well with Redwings and the species often flock together.
